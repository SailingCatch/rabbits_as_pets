A data story

Pet or pest? Keeping a rabbit in Australia

Australians love their pets, but very few keep a rabbit. The reason lies in the paddock, not the living room: the same animal that sits on a child's lap is the country's most costly pest.

  • 73% of Australian households have a pet
  • 1 in 65 households keeps a rabbit
  • 600 million wild rabbits at their peak, in 1950

Part one

The pet

01 · A nation of pet lovers

We love pets more than ever

Nearly three in four Australian households now have a pet, up from six in ten before the COVID-19 pandemic. There are about 31.6 million pets in the country, more than the 27.3 million people.

Dogs, cats, fish… and hardly any rabbits

Dogs and cats lead the way. Only 3% of households keep small mammals, and about half of those keep rabbits: roughly 1.5 homes in every 100.

So why so few? Part of the answer is how much space and care a rabbit needs. The bigger part is history: in Australia, the rabbit was a pest long before it was a pet.

Part two

The pest

02 · A plague

From a few rabbits to 600 million

In 1859 a Victorian grazier released rabbits so he could hunt them. Within about 70 years they covered 70% of the continent, the fastest known invasion by a mammal anywhere in the world.

A fence across a state

Between 1902 and 1907 Western Australia built three rabbit-proof fences to stop rabbits spreading west. Today wild rabbits are reported on both sides of every one of them.

03 · The virus war

Every new virus knocks them down. They come back.

Fences didn't work, so Australia turned to disease. Myxomatosis in 1950, then rabbit calicivirus in 1995. Each worked for a while, until the rabbits that survived bred a tougher population.

RHDV2: the latest wave

A new strain, RHDV2, arrived in 2015 and crossed the continent in 18 months. At six long-term monitoring sites, rabbit numbers fell by an average of 64%.

04 · Still everywhere

Wild rabbits live where people live

Rabbits are still reported across the south of the country, in farmland, bush and suburbs, which is exactly where most pet rabbits live too.

Part three

Back to the pet

05 · The rules

1 in 5 Australians can't legally keep a rabbit

Because rabbits are such a costly pest, the states treat them differently from dogs and cats. In Queensland, home to about a fifth of all Australians, keeping a pet rabbit is illegal.

Banned in Queensland

In Queensland keeping a pet rabbit is illegal under the Biosecurity Act 2014. South Australia requires an escape-proof cage by law, and the ACT has a mandatory code of practice. Everywhere else, domestic rabbits can be kept.

06 · The risk at home

The viruses meant for wild rabbits reach pets too

The viruses released to control wild rabbits kill pet rabbits as well, and insects can carry them from wild rabbits to a backyard hutch. Vaccines protect pets against calicivirus, but no myxomatosis vaccine is registered in Australia.

07 · When it goes wrong

When a small pet ends up in a shelter

The RSPCA doesn't count rabbits on their own. It groups them with other small pets such as guinea pigs, ferrets, mice and birds, so these charts show that whole group.

More than 6,000 a year

RSPCA shelters took in 6,267 small pets in 2024–25. New South Wales, Victoria and Queensland received the most.

08 · Why we love them

Company, calm, and a reason to get up

For all the rules and risks, people keep small pets for simple reasons: company, a hobby, a calmer day. Since 2022 far more owners say their small pet helps their mental health.

“I can have a reassuring cuddle with my ferret and a chat when I feel a bit lonely.”

Small mammal owner, Tasmania

“They have helped improve my mental health because I'm stuck at home all day.”

Bird owner, Queensland

Thinking of getting a rabbit?

  • Check your state's rules. Pet rabbits are illegal in Queensland, and SA and the ACT have extra rules.
  • Vaccinate against calicivirus. It protects against the virus spread to control wild rabbits.
  • Keep it in escape-proof, insect-proof housing. There's no myxomatosis vaccine in Australia, so keeping insects out matters.
  • Never let it go. Released pet rabbits can join the wild population, and releasing them is against the law in states such as NSW and SA.
